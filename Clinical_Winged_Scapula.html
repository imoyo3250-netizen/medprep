Patient Case

A 22-year-old man presents with prominence of the medial border of his right scapula. He has difficulty pushing against a wall and lifting objects overhead. The problem developed gradually after a recent illness.

On examination, the medial border becomes more prominent when he pushes his hands against a wall. Scapular protraction and upward rotation are weak, suggesting serratus anterior dysfunction and possible long thoracic nerve injury.

🦴 Anatomical Diagnosis

Likely diagnosis: medial winging of the scapula due to serratus anterior weakness.

The serratus anterior holds the scapula against the thoracic wall and helps protract and upwardly rotate it. Weakness allows the medial border to project posteriorly, particularly during a wall push-up.

🧠 Relevant Anatomy

  • Serratus anterior: arises from the upper ribs and inserts along the medial border of the scapula.
  • Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7): supplies serratus anterior. Injury can cause medial winging and difficulty with overhead elevation.
  • Trapezius: supplied by the spinal accessory nerve (CN XI), with proprioceptive fibres from C3–C4; it stabilizes and rotates the scapula.
  • Spinal accessory nerve injury: can cause trapezius weakness, shoulder droop, weak shrugging and a different pattern of scapular winging.

🩺 Clinical Examination

  1. Inspect both scapulae at rest for asymmetry, medial-border prominence and shoulder droop.
  2. Ask the patient to push against a wall; medial winging may become more obvious.
  3. Assess scapular protraction and upward rotation.
  4. Compare shoulder elevation on both sides.
  5. Test trapezius function with shoulder shrugging and resisted scapular retraction.
  6. Ask about trauma, surgery and other possible causes of nerve dysfunction.

⚠️ Clinical Features

  • Prominence of the medial border of the scapula, especially during a wall push-up.
  • Weakness of scapular protraction.
  • Difficulty or fatigue during overhead arm elevation due to impaired upward rotation.
  • Shoulder discomfort may occur but is not required for the diagnosis.
  • An isolated long thoracic nerve lesion is motor; sensory loss is not a typical defining feature.

🔍 Differential Diagnosis — Medial vs Lateral Winging

  • Medial winging: classically associated with serratus anterior paralysis due to long thoracic nerve injury.
  • Trapezius weakness: associated with spinal accessory nerve injury; look for shoulder droop and weak shrugging.
  • Rhomboid weakness: dorsal scapular nerve injury can impair scapular retraction and contribute to scapular malposition.

Use the pattern of winging with history and muscle testing; appearance alone does not prove the exact nerve lesion.

🚨 Complications

  • Persistent scapular instability or winging.
  • Reduced endurance and difficulty with overhead activities.
  • Shoulder discomfort and fatigue during repetitive movements.
  • Long-term functional limitation if the underlying nerve or muscle problem does not recover.

🛠️ Management

  • Identify and address the cause, such as traction injury, surgery-related nerve injury or inflammatory neuropathy.
  • Physiotherapy focused on scapular control, range of motion and appropriate strengthening.
  • Modify aggravating activities while maintaining safe shoulder movement.
  • Monitor recovery; consider specialist assessment when weakness is severe, persistent or diagnostically uncertain.
  • Surgery may be considered in selected persistent cases after specialist assessment and conservative treatment.

💡 Key Learning Point

Medial scapular winging should prompt assessment of serratus anterior and the long thoracic nerve.

Winging that becomes obvious during a wall push-up is a classic clue. Trapezius weakness from spinal accessory nerve injury has a different pattern, often with shoulder droop and weak shrugging.

🎯 Quick Clinical Questions

1. Which muscle is classically paralysed in medial winging of the scapula?

Serratus anterior. It normally holds the medial border of the scapula against the thoracic wall.

2. Which nerve supplies serratus anterior?

Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7). Injury can weaken protraction and upward rotation.

3. Which bedside manoeuvre can make medial winging more obvious?

Pushing against a wall. This loads serratus anterior and can accentuate the medial-border prominence.

4. Which nerve is associated with trapezius paralysis?

Spinal accessory nerve (CN XI). Look for shoulder droop and weak shrugging.

5. Is sensory loss a typical defining sign of an isolated long thoracic nerve lesion?

No. The long thoracic nerve is motor to serratus anterior.

6. Why can serratus anterior paralysis impair overhead elevation?

It weakens upward rotation and stabilization of the scapula. Normal scapular movement is needed for full arm elevation.
